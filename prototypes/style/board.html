Director's Cut: art direction board

The same two scenes in three film stocks. Top row of each column is K (Meanwhile...), bottom row is L (The Tight Edit). Click a K frame or press Space to cut all three at once; L loops one take. Pick a look before content work starts Saturday.

Recommended  1 · Nitrate, the silent era: toned black and white, a 4:3 gate, one stencil red that only ever means danger.

Why. Both games are native to that era. Cross-cutting was invented for the last-minute rescue, which is K's whole loop, and the timed stunt gag (safe, piano, anvil) is the silent comedy's set piece, which is L. Toned black and white is also the strongest way to make mismatched AI assets look like one film: their colours disappear, only value is left, and the material and grade control value. It reads as film at thumbnail size before anyone reads a word, and nothing in it needs Lumen, so the Web build keeps almost all of it.

Main risk. It can read as a cheap "old film" filter, or go grey and muddy so the hero and threats blur together. The guard is a strict value ladder (hero paper-white, heavies ink-black, the set in the middle), a crisp image with grain kept subtle and scratches rare, colour per location through toning, and red reserved for danger.

Why not 2 · Split Screen. The best match for K's mechanic, but the weakest unifier: generated textures keep their own colours, and its warm bounce light is Lumen, which the Web build doesn't have. In a thumbnail it looks like a well-graded default Unreal scene.

Why not 3 · Cut Paper. Unifies best and costs nothing on Web, but it reads as graphic design before it reads as film, every generated mesh needs its materials recoloured by hand, and lumpy AI geometry shows in flat shading.

Dropped before drawing: the 1980s VHS B-movie from the brainstorm. VHS noise and synthwave neon are the most common retro filter in jams and the default palette of generic AI art.

Thumbnail test: the itch.io grid, at about the size a judge sees it

1 · Nitrate pick

Silent era, 1915–1927 · 4:3 gate · toned black and white · stencil red and gold

Each location has its own toning (night blue, lamplight amber, dusk green). The hero is the brightest thing on screen, the heavies are ink, and the only saturated pixels are hand-stencilled: red for danger, gold for the hero and the payoff.

The cut
Hard cut on the press frame, one white flash frame, a 3-frame splice jump, a projector clack. A "Meanwhile..." intertitle card sits over the lower third for a second while play continues. L ends each take with an iris-out on the star.
Unifies AI assets by
Throwing colour away. Every generated texture becomes a grey value, the master material squeezes it into its role's band, and toning adds colour back under our control.
The Web build loses
Almost nothing: highlight bloom softens and the window light beams become flat cards. Grain and flicker are a UI overlay on both builds, because Film Grain isn't supported on the mobile renderer.

Mid-century title sequence, 1955–1962 · 16:9 · flat colour fields, ink shapes, two-tone shading

The game looks like the opening titles of a late-50s thriller: a flat colour field per location, ink-black heavies, a paper-white hero with a vermilion hat, hand-cut lettering.

Only your taste can settle these

  1. Yes or no on Nitrate? If no, which column, and what put you off?
  2. All black and white, or one colour payoff? For example, the Director's Cut medal screen or K's finale bursting into two-strip colour. It would be one extra post-process setting, but it isn't in scope until the fun gate passes.
  3. 4:3 gate with the HUD in the side margins, or full 16:9? 4:3 is period-true and sells "film" in the thumbnail; it costs a quarter of the screen width.
  4. Film dirt: as shown, calmer, or stronger? Grain, flicker, the odd scratch, gate weave.
  5. The hero: the flat-cap everyman drawn here, or another silhouette? Avoid anything that reads as one famous silent star.
